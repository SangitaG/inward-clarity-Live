When the Way You’re Operating
No Longer Serves You

Sometimes nothing obvious has gone wrong.

You may still be doing what you have always done: showing up, taking care of what needs to be done, working hard, being dependable, managing responsibilities, being there for the people who matter to you.

From the outside, life may appear to be working. And yet something about the way you are experiencing it no longer feels the same.

What once felt satisfying may feel less fulfilling. What once gave you a sense of security may now feel restrictive. What helped you feel connected may leave you depleted. What motivated you before may require more effort to sustain.

You may wonder why something that worked so well for so long no longer gives you what it once did.

The question may not simply be whether the way you are operating still works.

Does it still serve you?

The ways we operate develop for a reason

Over time, we find ways of thinking, responding, relating, achieving, and making decisions that help us navigate life. Often, they also help us meet something important within ourselves.

We may work hard because achievement gives us a sense of security or possibility. We may become highly dependable because being needed gives us a sense of connection or value. We may accommodate others because maintaining harmony helps us feel close to the people we care about. We may plan carefully because knowing what comes next gives us a sense of stability.

These are ways we learned to meet needs that genuinely mattered to us. And for a particular season of life, they may have served us very well.

But seasons change

We grow older. Relationships change. Responsibilities shift. Careers evolve. Children grow. Priorities change.

We change too.

Yet some of the ways we learned to navigate life become so familiar that we continue them without giving them much thought. We keep achieving, accommodating, taking responsibility, planning, saying yes.

Not necessarily because we have consciously decided this is still how we want to live. It is simply what we know how to do.

The season may have changed, while the way we are trying to meet our needs has stayed the same.

What you need may still matter

You may still want connection, security, contribution, belonging, recognition, stability, or independence.

But the way you have been meeting those needs may no longer give you the experience it once did.

If being dependable helped you feel valued and connected, being available to everyone may no longer be a fulfilling way to experience that connection. If achievement helped you create security and possibility, continually reaching for the next accomplishment may no longer give you what you are looking for. If keeping things under control helped you feel safe during an uncertain period, trying to anticipate and manage everything may now leave you exhausted.

Or what you need may have changed too.

What you need may still matter. But the way you've been trying to fulfill that need may no longer serve you.

Friction can tell us something

We may feel restless even though things are going well. We may resent responsibilities we once willingly accepted, feel less motivated by accomplishments that once mattered deeply, or become exhausted from being the person everyone can count on.

It is easy to turn that discomfort back on ourselves.

Why can't I just be grateful? Why am I finding this so difficult? Why don't I feel satisfied?

But friction doesn't always mean something is wrong.

It can be information.

It can draw our attention to a gap between the way we are operating and what we need from this season of our lives.

Becoming curious about what is underneath

Before changing what we're doing, it can be useful to understand why it has mattered to us.

What does this way of operating give me? What need has it helped me meet? Does it still give me what I need now?

And then perhaps the more important question:

What do I need in this season of my life?

These questions aren't asking us to reject the way we have lived. They help us understand it. Once we understand what a familiar way of operating has been doing for us, we may begin to see other ways of meeting the same need.

We can honor what served us and still evolve

When we recognize a familiar pattern, it can be tempting to look back and wonder why we didn't change it sooner.

But it served something.

It may have helped us build a career, create stability, raise a family, find belonging, develop confidence, or get through a difficult period.

We don't have to make those ways of operating wrong in order to choose differently now. We can appreciate what they allowed us to create and recognize that our lives may be asking something different of us today.

From there, a different question becomes possible:

How do I want to meet this need now?

What served one season does not have to define the next

The ways we operate can become so familiar that they begin to feel like who we are. But they are not necessarily the whole of who we are. They may simply be ways we learned to navigate life and meet important needs.

We can recognize what has served us, understand what we need now, and become more intentional about how we meet those needs in the life we are living today.

What you need may still matter. How you fulfill it can evolve.
