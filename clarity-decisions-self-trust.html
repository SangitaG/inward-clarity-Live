Clarity, Decisions & Self-Trust

Sometimes we know exactly what we want to do.

And sometimes we don't.

We think about a decision from one direction, then another. We imagine what might happen. We consider what other people might think. We remember what worked before. We question whether we're being realistic, responsible, too cautious, or not cautious enough.

The more we think, the less clear we can become.

It can feel as though we need to think harder. But sometimes what we need is not more thinking.

We need greater clarity about what is influencing our thinking.

What influences our decisions

When we make a decision, we bring many things to it.

Our experiences, our expectations, what we've learned, what we believe we should do, what others expect of us, what feels familiar, what we're afraid might happen, and what we hope will happen.

Somewhere among all of that is what matters to us.

When these influences become intertwined, it can be difficult to tell one from another.

We may say, I don't know what I want.

But the question isn't only What do I want?

We can also become curious about what is influencing the way we are thinking about what we want.

Clarity doesn't always give us an answer

Clarity of thought doesn't necessarily mean suddenly knowing exactly what to do. Sometimes clarity is simply being able to see what is there.

What am I assuming? What am I afraid of? Whose expectations am I carrying? Am I choosing this because it is what I want, or because it is familiar? What am I trying to accomplish? How do I want to experience this?

When we can see more clearly what is influencing us, we have more room to discern.

Not everything we're thinking needs to carry the same weight. Not every fear needs to determine what we do. Not every expectation needs to become our expectation. And not everything that served us before needs to decide what comes next.

Clarity creates room for discernment

Discernment allows us to consider what matters now.

We can begin to distinguish what truly matters to us from the other things influencing our decision.

We can consider the circumstances in front of us while also listening more carefully to ourselves.

From there, we can ask a different kind of question:

What choice is most aligned with what I truly want to experience or accomplish?

That doesn't guarantee certainty.

It doesn't guarantee the outcome we hope for.

But it changes how we make the decision.

Instead of simply reacting to everything influencing us, we can choose more intentionally.

And then we experience what follows

Every choice gives us information.

Sometimes things unfold as we hoped. Sometimes they don't. We may discover something we couldn't have known before making the decision, or realize we would choose differently now.

None of this necessarily means we made the wrong choice. It means we have something new to learn from.

We can notice what happened. How we experienced it. What mattered more than we realized. What we might carry forward and what we might change.

Then, when another decision comes, we meet it with a little more awareness than we had before.

This is one way self-trust grows

Self-trust isn't necessarily the confidence that we will always know the right thing to do. It isn't the promise that every choice we make will lead to the outcome we want.

It can be something quieter.

The growing knowledge that we can meet ourselves and our lives thoughtfully.

That we can pause. That we can become aware of what is influencing us. That we can discern what matters. That we can make an intentional choice.

And that whatever follows, we can reflect, learn, adjust, and choose again.

Over time, this can change our relationship with ourselves.

We may find ourselves needing less certainty before we move. We may become less afraid of getting every decision exactly right.

And we may begin to trust not because we always know what will happen, but because we have learned that we can meet what happens.

Perhaps that is what greater self-trust gives us.

Not certainty about life.

Greater confidence in our ability to engage with it.
